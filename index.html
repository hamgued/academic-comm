3 Interpersonal communication, communication accommodation, Grice's Maxims

 

What are interpersonal communication skills?

Interpersonal communication skills are the abilities we use to interact and communicate with each other. Skills like these include both verbal (spoken or written words) and non-verbal (body language, facial expressions, gestures) ways of sharing information, feelings, and ideas.

Being aware of these skills is important especially in academic, professional, and social settings. They help us build and maintain relationships, work effectively in teams, solve problems, and understand others better.

Examples of interpersonal communication skills

Active listening

Active listening means paying attention when someone is speaking and involves:

  • Focusing on the speaker without interrupting
  • Showing interest through eye contact and nodding
  • Asking questions to clarify meaning
  • Giving verbal feedback like “I see” or “That makes sense”
  • Showing empathy (understanding how the speaker feels)

Verbal communication

This is the ability to express your thoughts clearly and confidently using spoken or written language and includes:

  • Choosing the right words and tone for the situation (=understanding linguistic registers)
  • Speaking with a suitable tone and volume
  • Using accurate grammar
  • Being polite and respectful
Example:
In a presentation, you can express your ideas clearly and use connective phrases like 'In conclusion…' or 'Let me give an example…' to guide your audience.
Example:
During a group discussion, you listen carefully to your classmates' opinions and respond with 'That’s a good point.'

Non-verbal communication

Non-verbal communication refers to the messages we send without words and includes:

  • Facial expressions (smiling, frowning...)
  • Eye contact (looking at the other person)
  • Gestures (hand movements)
  • Posture (how you sit or stand)
  • Personal space and physical distance
  • Time
Example:
When someone is speaking, you show you’re interested by nodding and maintaining eye contact.

Teamwork

Teamwork is the ability to work well with others to complete tasks together and achieve a shared goal. Teamwork involves:

  • Sharing ideas and listening to others' ideas
  • Expressing opinions and respecting differing opinions
  • Identifying and helping solve problems together
  • Being reliable and cooperative
Example:
In a group project, you contribute on time and help your teammates when they need support. You actively attend team meetings, share your ideas, and expression your opinions.

Social skills

Social skills help us build and maintain positive relationships. Social skills include:

  • Being friendly and respectful
  • Understanding social rules and customs
  • Adapting to different people and situations
  • Showing kindness and patience

Example:
When meeting someone from another culture, you show interest in their personal background and avoid making assumptions; individuals are rarely, if ever, stereotypical representatives of their culture (see e.g. this about some Critique of Hofstede's cultural dimensions).

Improving your communication skills

You can develop your interpersonal communication skills time. Here are some basic ways to do this:

  • Practice regularly: Join group activities, speak in class, or participate in discussions.
  • Ask for feedback: Teachers, classmates, or friends can help you understand your strengths and areas where you need to improve.
  • Observe others: Watch how skilled communicators speak and behave.
  • Learn from experience: Reflect on what went well and what you could have done better after each interaction.

What affects (interpersonal) communication?

Communication is influenced by numerous factors, such as:

  • Personality: Some people are more outgoing, some are more reserved.
  • Culture: Different cultures have different communication styles.
  • Context: The situation (formal or informal) affects how we speak (=linguistic register). We speak differently with close friends than with our colleagues.
  • Mood: Our emotions affect how we communicate and express ourselves.

 

Communication Accommodation Theory (CAT) explains how people change the way they speak and behave to better connect with others. It was developed by Howard Giles, a social psychologist. The theory helps us understand how communication can be adjusted depending on the situation, relationship, and goals.

There are two main ways people adjust their communication:

Convergence

This means making your communication style more similar to the other person’s. For instance, you might:

  • Speak more slowly or clearly
  • Use  similar vocabulary or expressions
  • Match their tone or level of formality
  • Match their non-verbal expressions
Why?
Converging may help build rapport, show respect, or make the other person feel more comfortable.

Divergence

This means making your communication style more different from the other person’s style. You might:

  • Use your own accent or dialect more strongly
  • Avoid using the other person’s slang or expressions
Why?
Diverging helps emphasise your identity, maintain distance, or express disagreement.

Is communication accommodation always intentional?

Not always. Communication accommodation often happens unconsciously. This means people adjust their speech, tone, or body language without realising it at first.

For instance:

  • You might naturally speak more slowly when talking to someone who is not fluent in your language.
  • You may start using similar expressions or gestures as your friends or classmates over time.
    • This is an example of you creating a shared 'communicative' culture among your classmates. When people spend time together, they often begin to use shared communication styles.

This unconscious adjustment helps people feel more connected and understood. It’s a natural part of human interaction and shows how flexible and adaptive communication can be.

Why this important for students?

Being aware communication accommodation can help you:

  • Communicate more effectively with people from other backgrounds and cultures
  • Adapt your communication style in academic and professional or other social settings
  • Be more aware of how others might perceive your communication choices

 

The Cooperative principle is a concept that helps explain how people communicate effectively in social situations. It was introduced in the mid-1970s by Paul Grice, a philosopher of language.

The basic idea is simple: in most conversations, speakers and listeners cooperate with each other. That is, they try to make their contributions informative, truthful, relevant, and clear so that communication would work smoothly. Grice suggested that people often follow certain guidelines or expectations, called Grice’s Maxims, to help make communication successful.

Grice’s maxims are principles that describe how to communicate effectively and cooperatively. Observing them is a good basic rule of thumb for most workplace and business communication contexts.

Here are the four maxims:

  1. Maxim of quantity
    ➤ Give the right amount of information — not too much, not too little.
    Example: Don’t give unnecessary details in a meeting; just share what’s needed.
  2. Maxim of quality
    ➤ Be truthful. Don’t say things you know are false or that you don’t have evidence for.
    Example: In an academic report, only include facts you can support with data or sources.
  3. Maxim of relation/relevance
    ➤ Be relevant. Say things that relate to the topic or purpose of the conversation.
    Example: If the discussion is about project deadlines, don’t start talking about weekend plans.
  4. Maxim of manner
    ➤ Be clear. Avoid confusing language, ambiguity, or overly complex explanations.
    Example: Use simple, direct language when giving instructions.

These maxims are not strict rules, but rather helpful principles that describe how people usually communicate when they are trying to be cooperative.

Why is the Cooperative principle useful?

Understanding and applying Grice's maxims can help improve communication in academic, workplace, and everyday situations. The maxims can:

  • Make your messages easier to understand
  • Help avoid misunderstandings
  • Show respect for your listener’s time and attention

Note: maxims not always followed consciously

You should note that people don’t always consciously follow the maxims. Rather, they are often followed automatically or intuitively, especially in familiar or routine conversations. However, in some cases, speakers intentionally break (or flout) the maxims. When this happens, the listener usually understands that the speaker is not being literal and looks for a deeper or hidden meaning. This kind of communication relies on shared knowledge and context.

Examples of Grice’s Maxims in Academic Writing

1. Maxim of Quantity – Give enough information, but not too much

  • Academic writing must be clear, concrete, and consistent, avoiding vague phrases like “within the framework of” and overly complicated structures like “evaluation is carried out” → “is evaluated”.
  • Writers are expected to summarise and paraphrase source material, showing understanding without overloading the reader.

2. Maxim of Quality – Be truthful and evidence-based

  • Academic writing is evidence-based. Claims must be supported by references to previous research, such as:
    • “Previous studies have shown that…”
    • “According to the 1957 Annual Medical Report…”

3. Maxim of Relation – Be relevant

  • The structure of an academic paper or repot must be coherent, with each paragraph and chapter logically connected.
  • Writers are advised to avoid rhetorical questions and instead use direct, relevant statements:
    • “Is the welfare system good or not?” → “It is important to consider the effectiveness of the British welfare system.”

4. Maxim of Manner – Be clear and orderly

  • Academic writing avoids spoken-language expressions and uses precise vocabulary.
  • The Academic Phrasebank provides lists of linking phrases to help writers organise their ideas clearly:
    • “In addition,” “However,” “For example,” “Therefore,” etc.

4. Emails, meetings, collaborative writing, peer feedback

 

Email communication is an essential part of everyday professional communication. Emails allow for asynchronous communication in a way other forms of communication might not. Additionally, emails allow for more structured communication, which can be beneficial in professional settings.

Email communication can also be challenging and complex, especially for professional purposes. This is because sending professional emails requires careful planning, writing, and reviewing. Emails can be more convenient, flexible, and traceable than phone calls or meetings, but they can also be more impersonal, ambiguous, and delayed. Professional emails can convey a lot of information and details, but they can also be overwhelming and confusing.

To write effective emails, it is important to follow some best practices and tips that can improve the communication process and outcome. These tips include defining the audience, choosing the subject line, composing the body, and closing the message.

Defining the audience means knowing who the recipients are, what their needs and expectations are, how they prefer to communicate, and what tone and style to use. Choosing the subject line means writing a clear and concise summary of the message’s purpose and content, using keywords and phrases that capture the attention and interest of the recipients.

Composing the body means organizing the message into logical paragraphs or sections, using headings and bullet points to highlight key points, using simple and precise language, avoiding spelling and grammar errors, using appropriate salutations and sign-offs, and adding attachments or links if necessary.

Closing the message means ending with a polite and positive note, expressing gratitude or appreciation for the recipients’ time and attention, requesting or confirming any action items or deadlines, and providing contact information or alternative ways to communicate if needed.

Meetings are a vital part of professional communication. Meetings allow for synchronous, face-to-face interpersonal communication. They also allow for more collaborative and creative communication, which can be beneficial in professional projects.

Meetings can also be difficult and time-consuming, as they may require lots planning, conducting, and following up. They can be more engaging, productive, and satisfying than email messages or phone calls, but they can also be more disruptive, inefficient, and frustrating, especially if not planned well. Meetings can facilitate a lot of discussion and decision-making, but if conducted poorly, they can also generate conflict and confusion.

To conduct good meetings, it is important to follow some best practices and tips. These tips include setting the agenda, inviting the participants, leading the meeting, and closing the meeting.

Setting the agenda means defining the purpose and objectives of the meeting, outlining the topics and tasks to be covered, allocating time and resources for each item, sharing the agenda with the participants in advance, and requesting feedback or suggestions from the participants.

Inviting the participants means selecting the right people to attend the meeting, considering their roles, responsibilities, and contributions, sending them clear and timely invitations, confirming their attendance and availability, and providing them with relevant information and materials.

Leading the meeting means starting and ending the meeting on time, welcoming and introducing the participants, reviewing the agenda and setting the ground rules, facilitating the discussion and participation of all attendees, managing any conflicts or issues that arise, and keeping track of time and progress.

Closing the meeting means summarizing the main points and outcomes of the meeting, assigning or confirming any action items or responsibilities, setting or agreeing on any deadlines or follow-ups, thanking the participants for their involvement and input, and soliciting feedback or evaluation from the participants.

Collaborative or team writing is a common, important practice in professional settings. It involves working with others to produce a shared written document or project. This can have many benefits, such as increasing creativity, productivity, quality, and learning. However, collaborative writing also poses  challenges, such as managing conflicts, coordinating tasks, maintaining consistency, and ensuring accountability.

Following best practices and tips may help. These are, for instance, defining the purpose, choosing the team members, establishing the roles and responsibilities, planning the workflow, and reviewing and editing the final document.

Defining the purpose means clarifying the goals, objectives, and expectations of the writing project, as well as the audience, context, and format of the document.

Choosing the team members means selecting people who have relevant skills, knowledge, experience, and interest in the topic, as well as good communication and collaboration skills.

Establishing the roles and responsibilities means assigning specific tasks and duties to each team member, such as researching, drafting, editing, proofreading, formatting, or presenting.

Planning the workflow means creating a timeline and schedule for completing the writing project, as well as deciding on the tools and methods for communicating, sharing, and storing information.

Reviewing and editing the final document means checking the content, structure, style, and accuracy of the document, as well as proofreading for grammatical and other language mistakes and resolving any issues or disagreements among the team members. Reviewing the document can be done through peer feedback, self-evaluation, or external evaluation.

What Is Peer Feedback/Peer Assessment?

  • Peer feedback means that students give each other comments or suggestions on their work.
  • Peer assessment means students also give each other a grade or evaluation based on agreed criteria.

Why Is This Important in University?

In many universities — especially in Western education systems — students are expected to become independent thinkers and active participants in their learning. That includes learning how to:

  • Evaluate quality (not just receive grades)
  • Communicate ideas clearly
  • Give and receive criticism

Addressing Cultural Expectations

In some cultures, only the teacher’s opinion is considered valid. That’s understandable since teachers are respected experts. However, in university, especially in international or multicultural settings, learning is often collaborative. That means:

  • Everyone’s perspective can add value
  • Learning from peers can help you see your work differently
  • Giving feedback can help you understand the subject better

Think of it like a team sport: the coach (teacher) is very important, but players (students) also learn from each other during practice.

How Does It Work?

1. You share your work (e.g. a presentation, report, or project).
2. Your classmates read or watch it.
3. They give feedback using clear guidelines (e.g. what was strong, what could be improved).
4. You reflect on their comments and improve your work.

Sometimes, you also give a score based on a rubric (i.e. a list of criteria).

What If I Don’t Feel Comfortable Assessing Others?

That’s normal at first. However:

  • You’re not replacing the teacher but supporting your classmates.
  • You’re learning how to think critically and respectfully.
  • Teachers review and moderate peer assessments to ensure fairness.

Some benefits:

  • You learn to see how others work.
  • You improve your own work by seeing different approaches.
  • You build confidence in expressing your ideas.
  • You prepare for real-world teamwork, where feedback is constant.

7. Good academic practices

As you likely already know, as a university student, you are expected to be honest and produce high-quality academic work. This requires you to manage your time and dedicate enough time outside of classes to studying, reviewing materials, and completing coursework. 

That is, good academic practice requires you to spend time looking for and citing reliable sources and producing well-written work to report on your findings.

You are expected to use credible, reliable sources in all the coursework you produce. This means you need to spend time looking for and evaluating sources.

Remember that relying solely on search engines such as Google is not sufficient. Instead, you should use the LAB Primo system to find appropriate academic sources. 

You should also note that using an AI as your source is not good academic practice. In fact, LAB guidelines state the following:

AI applications or the content they generate cannot be used as scientific sources. AI does not consider ethical perspectives and is not a reliable source in  academic works. (LAB AI instructions 2025.)

To cite the sources you use in your coursework, you must provide in-text citations and compile a reference list as instructed in LAB's Thesis Guidebook (scroll down on the eLAB page to find the guidebook).

Although not explicitly covered in the coursebook materials, you are expected to learn to work independently, make and justify your choices (e.g. narrow down topics for coursework and later for your thesis), and spend time working outside of scheduled lesson time. 

Remember that you are a full-time student now. You may often need to dedicate up to 25 hours a week outside of scheduled lessons for studying.

To produce high-quality coursework at university, you must put in the effort. You should be aware that your teachers can generally tell whether a student has put sufficient effort into their coursework.

Regarding good written work, the absolute minimum is to always proofread your writing and ensure that you have produced a polished, professional document. Submitting messy documents is poor academic practice and unprofessional.

  • Analysis/to analyse: To examine something in detail and explain its meaning or structure. The process of breaking down complex information into smaller parts for understanding.
  • Category/to categorise: To group things based on their similarities or differences. The act of grouping items based on shared characteristics.
  • Citation/citing: To acknowledge the sources of information or ideas used in your work.
  • Classify: To arrange things/items into classes, groups, or categories according to certain criteria (e.g. shared characteristics).
  • Conclusion/to conclude: To summarize the main points or findings of your work and state the implications or recommendations.
  • Deductive: Reasoning that moves from general principles to specific conclusions. (Principle: All dogs are mammals. Premise: Pluto is a dog. Conclusion: Pluto is a mammal.)
  • Formulate: To express or create a plan, idea, or theory in a clear and systematic way.
  • Hypothesis/to hypothesise: To propose a tentative explanation or prediction based on observation or evidence. A testable prediction or proposed explanation for a phenomenon.
  • Inductive: To reason from specific cases or examples to general principles or conclusions. (Observation: I see a lot of snow on the ground. Pattern: Whenever there is snow, the temperature is below 0°C. Conclusion: The temperature is below 0°C today.)
  • Infer: To form a conclusion from evidence and reasoning.
  • Logical: To use clear and valid reasoning or arguments that are consistent and coherent.
  • Referencing: To provide the bibliographical details (e.g., author, year of publication, title, publisher…) of the sources that you have cited or consulted in your work.
  • Synthesis/to synthesise: To combine or integrate different sources, ideas, or perspectives into a new or coherent whole.
  • Theory: A well-supported explanation of observations or phenomena, often incorporating hypotheses and tested assumptions, and based on a vast body of scientific evidence. (Note: This is a word not to be used lightly.)
  • Thesis: The main argument or claim of your work, usually in one sentence. Or: Bachelor’s/Master’s thesis.
  • Method: The steps or procedures that you followed to conduct your research or analysis.

17. Source references/list of references

 

(Note: also commonly called in-text reference/in-text citation)

The term 'source reference' or 'in-text source reference' has been chosen at LAB UAS to refer to the identifying details about the used (cited) source material, which details are added within the text in an academic paper. It means the same as 'in-text citation', which is a common term in many English-language guides for academic writing.

Where an academic paper does not display a reference to a source, the person reading it assumes that the text is by the author or common knowledge in the field.

An example of a source reference, illustrating the Harvard system (name-year system):

Most of the tourist arrivals to Finland in 2019 were from European countries (Statistics Finland 2020).

In the above example, what is the source reference? It is this: (Statistics Finland 2020), indicating the author and the publication year of the cited publication. In this case, the source data was provided not by a person but an organisation named Statistics Finland. When the source material was created by a person, only his/her surname is mentioned. This type of a reference system is called the Harvard system, also called the 'name and year' system. As shown above, the name and the year are placed within brackets.

Currently, LAB Thesis Guidebook provides one option for entering source references: the Harvard system. However, the fields of study may allow other reference methods, too, such as the number reference system.

In brief, a reference entry according to the Harvard system includes:

  • round bracket (
  • surname of author/name of organization
  • year of publication
  • round bracket ).

More information and instructions for various cases are available in the LAB Thesis Guidebook and in the course materials on this Moodle platform.

What is a List of References (Anneli Rinnevalli)

The list of references contains detailed information about the sources of information that have been used (= cited). The purpose is to give the readers all the details they need to find those sources. It also gives credit to the authors whose ideas have been cited. The list is generally placed at the end of the written work (e.g. a report, an essay, or an article).

All the sources referred to in the text, and only those sources, must be included in the reference list. If any sources are cited in an appendix, they are not entered in the list of references but a separate list of references can be made for each appendix. Typically, the reference item details include the

  • name of the author(s)
  • year of publication
  • title of the publication
  • publishing information, e.g. name of publisher or publication channel (e.g. a journal), or, in the case of electronic resources (e.g. e-books, online databases, and websites), the URL or DOI and the date when the resource was visited.

More detailed information and guidelines are provided in the LAB Thesis Guidebook.

When using the Harvard system, a list of references is in alphabetical order according to the first letter of the first word in a reference item. Typically this word is the surname of the author or the name of an organization.

In the list of references:

Pears, R. and Shields, G. 2010. Cite them right. The essential referencing guide, 8th edition. Basingstoke, Hampshire, UK: Palgrave Macmillan.

  • surnames of the two authors: Pears and Shields
  • initials of the first names of the authors: R. and G. (Richard and Graham)
  • year when published: 2010
  • title of the book: Cite them right. The essential referencing guide
  • edition: 8th (not obligatory as the publication year specifies the edition)
  • publisher's location: Basingstoke, Hampshire, UK
  • publisher: Palgrave Macmillan.

In the text:

(Pears and Shields 2010) or (Pears & Shields 2010)

According to Pears and Shields (2010), the majority of students are searching for information via the Internet.

Journal articles (printed)

In the list of references:

Dawes, J. and Rowley, J. 1998. Enhancing the customer experience: contributions from information technology.  Management Decision, 36(5), 350-357.

  • surnames of the two authors: Dawes and Rowley
  • initials of the first names of the authors: J. and J.
  • year of publication: 1998
  • title of the article: Enhancing the customer experience: contributions from information technology
  • title of the journal: Management Decision
  • issue information (volume, part number, month): 36(5)
  • pages: 350-357.

In the text:

(Dawes and Rowley 1998) or (Dawes & Rowley 1998)

Dawes and Rowley (1998), in their article about enhancing the customer experience, predicted quite accurately the development of ...

Journal articles (online)

In the list of references:

Liu, B.C., Ivers, R., Norton, R., Boufous, S. Blows, S. and Lo, S.K. 2008. Review of professional training. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, 2, Ovid. Retrieved on 5 May 2020. Available at http://ovidsp.uk.ovid.com/spb/ovidweb.cgi

  • surnames of the six authors: Liu, Ivers, Norton, Boufous, Blows, Lo
  • initials of the first names of the authors: B.C., R., R., S., S., S.K.
  • year of publication: 2008
  • title of the article: Review of professional training
  • title of the journal: Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews
  • issue information (volume, part number, month): 2, Ovid
  • date when the material was read (viewed): Retrieved on 5 May 2020 (Note: the date is written in the modern English style as displayed here.)
  • webpage address (URL): http://ovidsp.uk.ovid.com/spb/ovidweb.cgi

In the text:

(Liu et at. 2008)

Observing the findings by Liu et al. (2008), the present study focused on ...

Note: et al. means 'among others'. Thus, only the first author's name is included and the rest are replaced by et al.

Referring to several pages on one website

Visiting several pages on one website could be compared to reading several articles in a journal or magazine. Each webpage has its own title and URL, perhaps a different author, too. 

The list of references must provide sufficient information for anyone wanting to find, with relative ease, and read the particular publications. As each webpage has a unique address, all the visited and cited webpages must be entered separately in the list of references. In other words, the exact location of the source must be provided. 

Thus, there may be several articles (webpages) published by the same author in the same year to include in a list of references. How can one differentiate them?

The solution is that a low case letter (a, b, c, d ...) is attached to the year of publication, for example:

  • Marimekko 2018a. Objectives & Strategy ...
  • Marimekko 2018b. History ...
  • Marimekko 2018c. News and Events ...
  • Marimekko 2018d. Annual Report 2017 ...

Below there is a sample from a report:

Marimekko will continue to invest in growth, creating even more attractive designs and products (Marimekko 2018a). Also testing and quality control will receive special attention during the coming years (Marimekko 2018c).

In the above sample, (Marimekko 2018a) refers to the webpage titled 'Objectives & Strategy' and (Marimekko 2018c) refers to the webpage titled 'News and Events'.

18. Quoting, summarising, paraphrasing

 

There are three kinds of notes the writer can make based on source materials:

  1. quotation
  2. summary
  3. paraphrase

Remember to keep track of the source so that you do not accidentally commit plagiarism. Always acknowledge the external sources clearly and apply the chosen techniques carefully.

See the examples below:

ORIGINAL TEXT

U.S. English and other such groups maintain that linguistic divisions have caused unrest in several countries, such as Canada and Belgium – though they generally fail to note that the countries where strife and violence have been most pronounced, such as Spain, are the ones where minority languages have been most strenuously suppressed.

The above text was published in the following book:

Bryson, B. 1990. The Mother Tongue: English & How It Got That Way. New York: Avon.

QUOTATION NOTE       

-cites the original word-for-word (do not over-use)

Referring to causes of unrest in many countries, Bill Bryson (1990, 240) claims that the countries where strife and violence have been most pronounced, such as Spain, are the ones where minority languages have been most strenuously suppressed.

Note 1: Quite commonly, quotations are indicated by adding quotation marks ("  ") around the quoted passage of text. However, the method to be used at LAB is to type the quotation in italics, as shown above.

Note 2: The numbers within brackets (1990, 240) in the above in-text reference (citation) are to be interpreted as follows: 1990 = the year of publication, 240 = the page number.

SUMMARY NOTE          

-reduces the length of a text, keeps the main points

The most violence between speakers of different languages occurs in countries where minority languages are the most strongly suppressed (Bryson 1990, 240).

Note 1: When summarizing, the writer must change the wording of the original text, as you see above. Copied sentences or phrases from original texts seldom succeed to make a good summary. Extra care must be taken to guarantee that the original core message remains the same.

Note 2: The text and numbers within brackets (Bryson 1990, 240) in the above in-text reference (citation) are to be interpreted as follows: Bryson = the author of the original text, 1990 = publication year, and 240 = the page number.

 PARAPHRASE NOTE

-rewrites the original using different language/wording, keeps the content

Bill Bryson (1990, 240) refers to U.S. English’s claim that having several languages in one country has led to many clashes, for example in Canada and Belgium. However, it is less seldom mentioned that the most violence has occurred in countries where minority languages are the most strongly suppressed, such as Spain.

Note 1: When paraphrasing, make sure you understand the original text completely. This is because in the process of paraphrasing the content matter, it easily happens that some meaning is lost or changed. This cannot happen, of course, when citing information or ideas created by someone else.

Note 2: When citing sources, you most often use two citing techniques: summarizing and paraphrasing. While reading, a good idea is to make notes of the original text in a few key words and then use the notes to write your own sentence(s).

On Quotations (Anneli Rinnevalli)

Quotations are exact, word-for-word copies of original texts created by other writers.

When and why are quotations used?

In general, writers should not quote a source unless its exact words matter to the point they are making. Writers may want to use quotations for the following reasons:

  • the exact original text is necessary to repeat before presenting an analysis, comments, arguments or objections
  • the original words are of primary evidence, for example an informant's words
  • the original words are unique or especially illuminating
  • the original material is particularly complicated and paraphrasing it would compromise clarity
  • it is necessary to convince the readers that no misinterpretations of the original material have happened.

How do you to quote correctly?

  • Quote sparingly, and when you quote, have a good reason to do so.
  • Never quote long passages of text.
  • Check that the quotation is identical to the original. You can use the ellipsis in square brackets […] to cut out unnecessary parts of the quotation.
  • Integrate short quotations into a sentence. Quotations of one sentence or a couple of sentences can be included in the paragraph or typed as a separate paragraph.
  • Longer quotations must be supported with comments from you.
  • Use quotations only to support and illustrate a point, not to make a point for you.

Remember that too many quotations will make readers wonder whether you have contributed any of your own ideas.

Most importantly, you must always reference the source of every quotation, paraphrase, and summary, both in your text and in the list of references.

 

Generally, when citing other authors, you write down only the main ideas (= summarize). In the process, you change the wording of the original text and write mostly in your own words (= paraphrase).

There are several techniques for paraphrasing:

1. Changing vocabulary by using synonyms

  • argues -> claims
  • eighteenth century -> 1700s
  • wages -> labour costs
  • economise -> save

Note: Do not attempt to paraphrase every word, since some have no true synonyms (e.g. demand, economy, energy) and some are specialist terms that should be used consistently for clarity.

2. Changing word class

  • explanation (n.) -> explain (v.). 

An example: The explanation for the recent development is the growing interest in adventure tourism. -> The growing interest in adventure tourism explains the recent development.

  • mechanical (adj.) -> mechanise (v.). 

An example: The process uses mechanical devices to separate the meat from bones. -> The process of separating the meat from bones is mechanized.

  • profitable (adj.) -> profitability (n.).

An example: These innovations should result in more profitable operations. -> These innovations should increase the profitability of operations.

3. Changing word order

An example: The best explanation for the UK origin of the industrial revolution may be demand factors.

->->-> Demand factors may be the best explanation for the UK origin of the industrial revolution.

Note that minimal changes, as illustrated in the above example, are not recommended. Usually, changing word order is combined with the techniques of summarizing and paraphrasing.

A summary has three principal requirements:

  1. It should be focused on the relevant aspects of the source text or texts.
  2. It should present the source material in an accurate fashion.
  3. It should condense the source material and be presented in the writer’s own words.

Summaries that consist of directly copied portions of the original rarely succeed. Such a summary may suggest that you can find potentially important information, but it does not reveal to what extent you have understood it. In addition, you may be engaging in plagiarism.

It is essential that you thoroughly understand the source material you are working with.

  1. First, skim through the text(s), noting in your mind the subheadings or try to divide the text into sections.
  2. Read the text(s) carefully, highlighting important information or taking notes.
  3. In your own words, write down the main points, but do not paraphrase specialised vocabulary or technical terms. Reorganise the structure if needed.
  4. Write down the key support points for the main topic, but include minor detail only if necessary.
  5. Check that the summary is accurate and that it includes all the important points, not changing the original meaning.

Make sure the summary flows smoothly; use enough transition devices and supporting detail. You do not want a collection of sentences that do not flow.

Most summaries have a sentence near the beginning that contains two elements: the source (in the examples below, the source is Melville 2006) and the main idea.

  • According to Melville (2006) ……………………. (main idea)
  • Melville’s paper (2006) on cognitive learning discusses …………… (main idea)
  • Melville (2006) states / claims / argues / maintains that……….. (main idea)
  • Melville (2006) suggests / asserts / hypothesizes / states / concludes that ………. (main idea)
  • In Melville’s article ‘Cognitive Learning’ (2006), ………………………….. (main idea)
